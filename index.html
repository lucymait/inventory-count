Product rationale

Why this works the way it does.

The problem

Store managers count stock in spreadsheets today: slow, inconsistent between shops, and clunky to do on the shop floor. A wrong count means running out of oat milk at 9am or over-ordering stock that spoils.

The brief: a lightweight count tool for a prioritised list of up to 10 items, hands-free (tapping, not typing) and error-tolerant (mistakes easy to fix). Auth, multi-store, dashboards and reporting were out of scope.

Who it's for

A store manager on the shop floor, phone in one hand, walking between the fridge and back of house, likely to be interrupted by customers or staff. Not someone at a desk with a spreadsheet. Every decision below follows from that.

Key decisions and why

  • One item per screen, in shelf order. Fewer decisions per screen means fewer mistakes. Items are grouped the way stock is physically stored, so staff walk the shop once.
  • Tap to count, never type. Large +, −, and +5 buttons work one-handed. +5 speeds up bulk items like cartons and cup sleeves.
  • Count each location separately. Milk lives in the fridge and back of house. Counting them separately and showing the total avoids the most common error: counting one spot and forgetting the other.
  • Catch mistakes as you go. The app flags anything below its par level as low stock, and asks for a double-check if a count is far higher than usual (an extra zero, for example).
  • Skip, don't block. If an item can't be reached, staff skip it and keep going. Skipped items are clearly marked on review, so nothing is silently missed.
  • Review before submit. One screen shows every count, low-stock flags and anything skipped. Tap to correct, then submit.
  • Progress is saved. If a customer interrupts or the app closes, the count picks up where it left off.
  • Turn the count into an action. Submitting shows suggested reorder quantities, because the count only matters if it drives the order.

What I left out, and why

  • Barcode scanning: slower than tapping for a short list of familiar items, and needs camera permissions and good lighting.
  • Login, multi-store and dashboards: out of scope in the brief, and only worth building once the count itself is fast and trusted.
  • Automatic ordering: suggested quantities first; a human approves the order until the data is proven reliable.

How I'd measure success

  • Primary: time to complete a count (target: under 10 minutes).
  • Accuracy: gap between counted stock and stock implied by sales and deliveries.
  • Outcomes: fewer stock-outs of key items, less waste from over-ordering.
  • Adoption: share of shifts completing a count, and how often items are skipped.

Next steps

Test it with store managers during a real shift, watch where they hesitate, and compare time and accuracy with the current process before adding anything new.
